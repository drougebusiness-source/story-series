# The Writers' Room: how we write (the craft bible)

Owned by Sloane, the Head Writer. Every script for JIC Precautions and the four story series (COMPLIANT,
The Last Draft, Roll for Fate, Driftborn) follows this guide. A copy lives in `story-series/WRITING.md` for
the episode generator; this file is the master copy, so change this one and re-copy it.

**The one rule:** every line has one job, which is to make them need the next line. Flat writing *reports*
what happened. Good writing *withholds* something, then pays it off later than they expect and in a way they
didn't see coming, but should have.

The owner's note that started this: "Claude writing can be too flat and straight to the point; human writing is
more artistic and flows like a story, with unexpected but also expected outcomes sometimes."

---

## 1. The hook (the first line, the first 2 seconds)

The first line must open a question the viewer can't answer yet. It starts *inside* the moment: no
throat-clearing, no "in a world where", no title card that explains.

Six hook shapes that work. Each one comes from a reel that's winning right now:

| Shape | How it works | Seen in |
|---|---|---|
| **The Rule** | State a strange law of this world, plus the one thing you must never do. The viewer knows it will be broken. | "The Visit" (rearend_stories): the dead come back for one day, and you must never tell them. |
| **You Are There** | "Imagine you're..." with a small, exact scene (where, what time, how many people), then one wrong detail. | "Don't Open the Doors" (3 AM Choices): the last subway car at midnight, three strangers. |
| **The Wrong Detail** | A normal scene with one thing that shouldn't be there, or something missing that should be. | The old woman is no longer in her seat. |
| **Flash-forward** | Open at the worst moment, then jump back. "Day 203. Three flashlights crossed the frozen yard." | JIC full videos (cold open) |
| **The Confession** | The narrator admits a mistake before we know what it is. "I had eleven cans of soup. I gave away the wrong one." | |
| **What If, by days** | An absurd premise, then a Monday-to-Sunday escalation told with a straight face. | "What if your dad woke up Jamaican" (puretheories) |

**Hook test:** would a stranger who is mid-scroll stop? Can you say in five words the question line 1
plants? If not, rewrite it.

**Write ten hooks and keep the best one, never the first.** The first idea is the one every other writer
(and every AI) also had.

## 2. Keeping them hooked: the open-loop engine

- **Always keep one question open.** Close a loop only after a bigger one has opened. "Will they get out?"
  should hand off to "Who is the girl?", which hands off to "Who is the message from?"
- **But / therefore, never "and then".** Each beat should cause the next one ("therefore") or turn against it
  ("but"). If beats are joined by "and then", it's a list, not a story.
- **Escalate every beat.** A beat must raise the stakes, start or shorten a clock, or flip what we believe.
  If it does none of these, cut it.
- **Two voices the viewer trusts, saying opposite things.** The driver says stay; the man says run; the girl
  says he never got on at any station. Now the *viewer* has to decide, and they lean in. This is the engine for
  every vote episode.
- **Misdirect openly.** Give the audience a suspect to watch (the man in the coat) while the real danger sits
  beside them, holding their hand.

## 3. Surprising but inevitable (the twist)

The owner asked for "unexpected but also expected outcomes." The craft name for this is *surprising yet
inevitable*: when the reveal lands, the viewer replays the story and sees it was always there.

- **Plant, then pay off.** Every twist needs three or more clues placed in plain sight *before* it lands. In
  "The Visit": the covered mirrors, the pancakes nobody had made since the accident, the dad memorizing a face,
  the dog hiding, one set of footprints. Each clue reads one way the first time and another way after.
- **Fair play.** Never pay off something you didn't plant. A twist from nowhere feels cheap; a planted twist
  feels like a gift.
- **Mix expected and unexpected.** About half the time, give the audience the outcome they predicted, by a
  route they didn't predict. The other half, flip it with the planted reveal. If every ending twists, twists
  become the expected thing.
- **One line turns everything over.** "It was never her visit. It was mine." Say it in as few words as
  possible, then **don't explain it**. Follow it with one quiet, human beat (a hug, a joke through tears), not
  a summary.

## 4. Rhythm and voice

- **Vary sentence length.** Let a long sentence build, clause after clause, while the light flickers and the
  knocking gets louder and nobody in the car moves. Then a short one. *Silence.* Fragments are spice, not the
  meal: a script made only of fragments ("Routine. Forty names. Order.") is the flat sound the owner hears.
- **Show the behavior, not the label.** Don't write "He loves order"; write "He squares the file's corners twice
  before he lets it go." Don't write "Dad was sad"; write "Dad kept staring at us, like he was trying to memorize
  a face."
- **Be exact.** Use a named place (Miller Pond), a number (100 meters ahead, Batch 4471), a brand of cereal, the
  time on the microwave. Exact details make made-up things feel true.
- **Use the senses.** Every scene gets one sound, one texture or one temperature: an ice-cold hand, the smell of
  a candle that's just gone out, the hum that stops.
- **Dialogue is subtext.** People don't say what they feel; they say what they want and hide the rest. Keep it
  short, let people interrupt each other, and give each character one line that reveals what they want.
- **A narrator with a pulse.** The narrator can be wry, scared or tender, but never neutral. Keep a straight face
  for comedy and stay calm for horror; calm horror is scarier.

## 5. Endings

- **Cliffhanger episodes** end on *new information plus a forced choice with a clock*, not on a mood. Both
  options must be tempting and both must cost something. The best choices set what the heart wants against what
  the head knows.
- **Complete stories** end on the turn (section 3), then one human beat. Never end on a moral, a summary or "and
  that's when everything changed".
- **JIC** endings land the lesson as a *consequence* the viewer just watched happen, then the kit line from
  STYLE.md.

## 6. The flat-writing ban list

Search every draft for these and rewrite each one you find.

- **Phrases:** "little did they know", "a chill ran down", "in a world where", "heart pounding", "couldn't help
  but", "eerie silence", "palpable", "testament to", "tapestry", "delve", "and that's when everything changed",
  "suddenly" (use it at most once per script), and "It's not X, it's Y" (at most once).
- **Naming the emotion** ("She was terrified"). Show what her hands do instead.
- **Explaining the twist** after it lands, or a character recapping what we just saw.
- **The rule of three, everywhere:** three adjectives, three beats, three of anything, over and over.
- **Every sentence the same length**, or every caption a two-word label ("Accused.").
- **Starting at the beginning** ("It was a normal day..."). Start at the most interesting second.
- **Polite stakes.** If nothing can be lost, nothing is watched.

## 7. Formats

**Story-series Reels** (35-75 s, 5-8 shots; each shot has a caption of 140 characters or fewer and usually a
spoken line):
- 0-3 s: hook (section 1).
- 3-15 s: the world, who these people are, and what is at stake, in concrete terms. This is the owner's rule:
  say *what* Wren is accused of, not just "Accused".
- Middle: two escalations and one turn, linked by but/therefore.
- Last 10 s: a reveal, then a choice with a clock. Each caption must make sense read on its own *and* pull the
  viewer into the next shot.
- **No teaser and no vote recap (owner, 2026-11-17: "it adds too much").** Episodes are 15-29 s, never over 30.
  Open straight on the hook. An episode after a vote picks up the choice *inside the story* in its first line: not
  "Option B won", but "You chose to copy the file. Now the chip is in his shoe, and Kols is searching everyone at
  the gate."
- Character entrances are shown, mouths move when people talk, and a world looks the same every time
  (story-series memory rules).

**JIC full videos** (12-15 min, second person, present tense, one place, Day 1 to Day N; STYLE.md is the
format):
- The cold open starts on the worst moment, then cuts back to Day 1.
- Open loops run across chapters: "That was the gas she'd need on day nine."
- Every day-jump ends on a small cliff.
- A real fact is a *plot turn*, never a lecture: the mistake happens first, the fact arrives as the
  consequence, and the fix happens on screen.
- The fact-check rules in STYLE.md still win over style, every time.

**JIC myth Shorts:** the hook is someone about to make the mistake. The myth is the trap. The truth lands at
about 70% through, and the final beat shows the right move.

## 8. The writer's loop (every script, every time)

1. **Find the spine:** the one question the piece runs on, and the one image people will remember.
2. **Write ten hooks** and pick the best.
3. **Beat sheet:** link the beats with but/therefore, mark where each open loop opens and closes, and list the
   plants for any twist.
4. **Draft fast.**
5. **Punch up:** read every line aloud for rhythm, kill everything on the ban list, swap labels for behavior and
   cut 15%.
6. **Score it from 1 to 5** on each of: hook stop-power · a new question at least every 10 s · surprise ·
   fairness (were the twists planted?) · specific details · feeling · pull of the ending. Rewrite any part that
   scores under 4. Write the scores in hq/notes/head-writer.md so we can compare them with views later.

## 9. Before and after (our own work)

COMPLIANT, episode 1 opening.
- *Before:* "Citizen 213. Grade-3 Records Clerk. He loves order." / "Batch 4471. Forty names. Routine."
- *After:* "Citizen 213 has deleted eleven thousand people. He has never once looked up." / "Batch 4471. Forty
  names. He'll be home by six." / "Name thirty-nine is his sister."

The first version tells us he's orderly. The second shows us what his order *costs*, puts a clock on it, and
plants the turn in the third line.

JIC myth hook.
- *Before:* "Myth: you should tape your windows before a hurricane."
- *After:* "Dana spent two hours taping big X's across every window. When the first gust comes, the tape won't
  stop the glass. It'll just make the pieces bigger."

## 10. Copyright and originality

Study winning reels for their *shapes* (section 1), never their words, characters or plots. Every story we post
is our own. Never copy more than a few words of anyone's script.
